I am considering "writing waterway" as another name for this site. The image starts with drops, which gather into streams, join into rivers, and eventually reach an ocean. It gives me a way to describe small observations becoming connected writing.

## A possible shape

- **Drops** are individual observations, questions, or fragments. A log entry could hold one.
- **Streams** connect a few drops around an idea. A developing note could do that work.
- **Rivers** bring several streams together into a longer argument or explanation. Articles could sit here.
- **The ocean** is the wider body of connected writing, including the work of other people that my thinking joins.

Maps could help readers follow a waterway, see where streams meet, and choose a direction. These are working associations. I have not decided to rename the site's sections.

## Related writing

Water already appears in writing about blogging and knowledge. These are useful comparisons, though none of the pieces below sets out this exact drops-to-ocean structure.

- [Mike Caulfield's The Garden and the Stream](https://hapgood.us/2015/10/17/the-garden-and-the-stream-a-technopastoral/) contrasts the chronological stream with a garden of connected, revisable ideas. That creates a useful challenge for my metaphor. I want a waterway where readers can trace connections and return upstream, without everything disappearing into a feed.
- [Robin Sloan's Stock and flow](https://snarkmarket.com/2010/4890/) describes short updates as flow and durable work as stock. He argues for using both. His distinction helps me ask whether a drop can contribute to something that remains useful after the day it was written.
- [Tom Critchlow's Building a digital garden](https://tomcritchlow.com/2019/02/17/building-digital-garden/) uses streams for Twitter, campfires for blogging, and gardens for deeper thinking. He also draws on Sloan's stock-and-flow idea. His categories describe different spaces for writing, while I am considering how pieces of writing join together.
- [Maggie Appleton's A Brief History & Ethos of the Digital Garden](https://maggieappleton.com/garden-history) traces the garden metaphor and explains its emphasis on connected ideas and ongoing revision. Her discussion of streams builds on Caulfield. It is a useful check that a new name still leaves room for those habits.

A quick search did not surface an established blogging framework called "writing waterway." That is a limited finding, and I am not claiming the phrase or the broader water analogy is original.

## What I need to test

A waterway suggests movement and connection. It also risks making every small thought sound as though it must become a large article, or making older writing seem permanently downstream. Some drops should stay small. Revision should still be possible anywhere in the system.

For now, I will keep this as a possible framing alongside [why I am building a digital garden](../articles/why-a-digital-garden.md). The next test is whether drops, streams, and rivers make the site easier for a new reader to understand.
